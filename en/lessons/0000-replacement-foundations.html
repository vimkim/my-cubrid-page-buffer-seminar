Three-hour route ↑

Replacement begins when useful storage runs out

A replacement algorithm chooses which resident item should give up its slot when an absent item needs one. Finite capacity creates the need; locality makes the choice consequential. Without replacement, a full pool must reject or wait for new requests, grow, or obtain space through another mechanism.

FIFO remembers arrival order; OPT chooses the item whose next use is farthest in the known future; exact LRU remembers the most recent access to every item; Clock approximates reuse with a circular hand and reference bits. OPT is a comparison oracle, not an implementable prediction of unknown requests.

For the first hand trace, use three frames and requests P, R, S, P, T. FIFO evicts P on T; exact LRU evicts R. The hit on P changed recency but not arrival order. For OPT, the answer requires the future requests. For Clock, it requires the current bits and hand. A policy name alone does not specify the whole state.

The detailed traces below supply every starting condition. They assume all resident items can be evicted; databases add ownership, dirty-page, and concurrency constraints. These are abstract algorithms, so no invented database call stack is attached.

Fast storage is useful; capacity is limited

A cache keeps a subset of data in storage that is faster to access. Registers, CPU caches, main memory, and persistent storage form a hierarchy with different capacity and access costs; the exact costs depend on the machine. Here we isolate one fast cache and a slower backing store.

A page is a named, fixed-size block of data. A frame is a reusable slot that holds one page. Page P can leave frame F0; F0 still exists and can hold T. A hit finds the requested page resident. A miss requires obtaining it from the backing store in this model.

Temporal locality means reusing the same data soon. Spatial locality means using nearby data; bringing a block may serve later nearby accesses. Our recurring story uses P and R repeatedly, interrupted by a scan of distinct pages S, T, U. P/R form the small working set: the pages repeatedly needed in this phase. Reuse is a workload property, not a promise that a policy will retain P and R.

For an invented serial cost example, lookup costs 1 unit and a miss adds 100 units. Ten requests with three misses cost 10 × 1 + 3 × 100 = 310 units. Hit counts alone omit policy bookkeeping, overlapping I/O, and contention; these units are not hardware measurements.

Three frames, an absent fourth page

Take a separate capacity snapshot: F0=P, F1=R, F2=S. T is absent. The cache has exactly three frames. Loading T while retaining all three resident pages cannot fit.

Replacement chooses a resident page to remove so its frame can hold the incoming page. With no replacement, the system needs another permitted outcome: bypass the cache, wait or refuse the request, or grow capacity if its contract allows it. Growth changes our fixed-capacity premise; waiting alone creates no space. There is no universal crash or data-loss conclusion.

For the following trace, every miss must load into the cache, growth and bypass are disabled, every resident page is immediately reusable, and the backing store keeps a valid copy. Removing a clean cached copy does not delete the backing page. In-use pages and modified data introduce further rules in the database bridge.

FIFO: remember arrival, ignore hits

FIFO evicts the earliest-loaded resident page. Its queue runs oldest → newest; hits leave it unchanged. Empty queue initially; serial admissions cannot tie. Predict the victim at request 7 (T), then track the return to P/R.

OPT / MIN: inspect the future

On a full-cache miss, OPT evicts the page whose next use is farthest away. A page never used again has next use ∞; ties choose the lowest frame number. Exact LRU: remember every access

Exact least-recently-used replacement evicts the resident page whose last access was earliest. The order runs least → most recent; move every accessed page to the most-recent end, including hits and loads. Start empty. Serial request numbers make recency unique. Predict how request 3 changes metadata without moving any page bytes, and who leaves at 7 and 8.

A map plus a doubly linked list can update an access in constant time, but every access still maintains exact order. Shared concurrent metadata also needs synchronization; constant time does not mean free.

Clock: keep a reference bit and a hand

Frames form a ring F0 → F1 → F2 → F0. Initially the hand points at F0 and bits are 000. Every hit sets that frame's bit to 1 and does not move the hand. An empty-frame load sets its bit to 1 and moves the hand to the following frame.

On a full-cache miss, inspect the hand's frame: clear a 1 bit and advance; at a 0 bit, evict and load, set its bit to 1, then advance once. Metadata is bits in F0/F1/F2 order; next hand. Predict request 7 from 111; F0. Which bits get cleared?

Request 7 clears all three 1 bits, returns to F0, and replaces P with T. The result is 100; F1. At 8 and 9 it replaces zero-bit frames F1 and F2. At 10 it again sweeps three 1 bits and replaces F0. Totals are 3 hits and 7 misses. This run matches FIFO, but that is not a general identity: a hit on a zero-bit page can give it another chance. One bit records use since the last clearing, not the exact order of uses; Clock approximates recency and a miss can inspect several frames.

Equal totals do not make policies equivalent

The main run gives FIFO 3/7, OPT 5/5, LRU 3/7, and Clock 3/7 hits/misses. At request 7 FIFO/Clock evict P while OPT/LRU evict S. At request 8, LRU loses P while OPT preserves it. A small working set can fit and still be displaced by scan traffic.

Separate counterexample, reset to empty: P R S P T R. With three frames, FIFO hits P and the final R (2 hits, 4 misses); LRU hits only P (1 hit, 5 misses). Before T, FIFO's oldest page is P, but LRU's oldest is R. Thus recent use does not guarantee future use, and exact LRU need not beat FIFO.

Random chooses a resident victim randomly, avoiding recency ordering but risking a hot page. A particular random outcome is not an expected performance result. LFU prefers the least frequently used page; it needs count, tie, and aging rules. Without aging, an old popular page can remain favored after the workload changes. Neither is a universal winner. Compare misses, bookkeeping, and the actual workload.

Transfer exercise: predict a sequence you have not traced

Reset all policies to the same empty three frames and use P R S R T P R. Use the same tie and Clock conventions. Before opening the explanation, write every hit/miss, victim, physical frame state, and policy metadata. Explain why request 6 differs between FIFO and LRU, and why Clock's bit on R does not preserve R through the whole run. No answer is stored or scored.

Reveal explanation and state

FIFO and Clock hit only request 4: 1 hit, 6 misses. LRU hits 4 and 7: 2 hits, 5 misses. OPT hits 4, 6, and 7: 3 hits, 4 misses. At request 5 both FIFO and LRU evict P. At request 6 FIFO evicts R while LRU evicts S; that difference decides whether request 7 hits. Clock’s request 5 sweep clears R’s bit, so request 6 can evict R. What carries forward

Keep the workload shape: repeated P/R requests interrupted by S/T/U scan traffic. Keep the distinction between a page identity and a reusable frame, and between policy metadata and page contents. The database bridge adds in-use and dirty-page constraints before applying this reasoning to real buffer pools.

The main trace's final physical frames are FIFO/Clock: R / U / P; OPT: P / R / U; LRU: U / P / R. These are four alternative histories, not one shared state. The later CUBRID worked example starts from its own source-derived larger-pool snapshot. Only the workload idea transfers; neither three-frame arithmetic nor exact-LRU movement is asserted for CUBRID.
