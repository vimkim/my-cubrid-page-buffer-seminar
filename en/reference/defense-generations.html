1 / 6 · Problem and invariant

Overlap needs two debts and one coherent image

A dirty page must survive frame reuse, while other callers still need access during slow I/O. Assume logged permanent P, mutable resident bytes, and writes that can outlast a critical section. Safety requires a coherent submitted image, WAL covering that image, and preservation of every later dirty obligation.

Invariant: Completing G may retire G's in-flight work; it must not retire a mutation made after G was captured. DIRTY records current propagation debt. FLUSHING records an outstanding image and excludes a competing flush.

Inference: Copying buys overlap with writers. It is not logically necessary if a different design excludes mutation for the full write. Coldness and zero fixes do not discharge durability debt.

2 / 6 · Mechanism and evidence

The copied bytes and their log boundary travel together

The helper assumes its caller has established eligibility and holds the BCB mutex. It marks FLUSHING, clears the old DIRTY, captures the image and its page LSA under protection, saves/clears the oldest-unflush bound, and unlocks before WAL and submission. The helpers explicitly preserve a newly set DIRTY on successful completion.

For G at LSA 120, durable WAL through 140 suffices even if the resident page later reaches 180. The oldest-unflush LSA selects checkpoint work; it is not the copied image's latest-log boundary. A field returning 40 → 41 → 40 does not erase the newer logged history or current dirty debt.

Verified mechanism: page_buffer.c:10727–10966 · flag helpers · canonical generation explanation.

The BCB mutex alone cannot stabilize bytes against a different WRITE owner: caller eligibility/latch ownership must exclude that actor during capture. The source comment on the flag helper explicitly explains tracking changes during flush; broader overlap rationale remains Inference.

Removing an axis can erase work

4 / 6 · Alternatives and costs

Safe alternatives move the cost

Teaching alternatives: Hold exclusive byte access through WAL and page write: coherent image, one generation at a time, but writers now wait for storage. Or use immutable/versioned page images and an explicit generation acknowledgement: preserve overlap, but manage version lifetimes and retire only acknowledged debt.

For page size B and k concurrent flushes, copying requires O(B) byte work per image and O(kB) staging space across active calls, with stack or DWB-slot ownership depending on the branch. WAL latency, encryption, DWB capacity and I/O time are additional costs. Exclusive serialization saves staging but adds an I/O-length protected interval; that is not a measured latency comparison.

Removal with no replacement: If a device reads mutable bytes while a writer changes two related fields, it may observe a mixed image. Keeping byte exclusion through the transfer is a valid replacement. Removing WAL ordering instead permits a data image whose explaining log is absent after a crash.

Removing only competing-flush exclusion also changes ordering: submit old G, submit newer G+1, let G+1 finish first and G finish last at the same home page. With no ordering replacement, old bytes can win. Serializing flushes or a version-aware ordered-write protocol avoids this constructed schedule.

5 / 6 · Concurrency and failure boundaries

WAL and DWB cover different failures

WAL orders recovery information before the logged page image. It does not by itself make a multi-sector page write atomic. Under a torn-write assumption, direct home writing can leave a page made of old and new sectors. DWB supplies a staged recovery image through its own write/recovery protocol; disabling DWB is not automatically incorrect if storage atomicity or another repair mechanism supplies that requirement.

DWB acceptance, completed home-volume writing, OS write return and durable storage are distinct boundaries. Neither the copied image nor FLUSHING certifies device persistence. Concurrent identity reuse must remain excluded while this protocol owns P.

Ordinary I/O failure restores dirty/oldest-LSA bookkeeping; early TDE/DWB-slot returns are the separate candidate VS-12, not a proven universal rollback. DWB ownership and pinned evidence.

Downstream mechanism: DWB staging write and synchronization precede home writes; acceptance need not flush a partial block; recovery requires a valid staged image. Loss of both images or ineffective storage synchronization remains outside this argument. Full-page WAL images are a primary-source alternative torn-page repair technique with extra WAL volume.

6 / 6 · Defense and falsifiability

D25 · Can equal bytes justify skipping the later flush?

G contains value 40 at LSA 120. A writer logs 41 then 40 and leaves DIRTY set at LSA 180. A proposed optimization clears DIRTY after G succeeds because the selected payload field matches. Which invariant would its proof need?

Challenge, then reveal the bounded answer

Payload equality is insufficient: the page header, logged history and dirty-run boundary differ. A safe optimization needs a complete equivalence and recovery argument under the same protection interval, plus an acknowledgement scheme that cannot erase later work. This source provides no such rule.

Objection: “WAL through 180 is already durable.” That establishes log availability, not propagation of the resident image. G still contains 120.

Discriminating observation: Instrument copy LSA, current LSA, flag transitions and submitted image identity. A newer mutation followed by DIRTY being cleared on old completion falsifies the split-debt claim. Crash/torn-write tests require stated storage fault assumptions; this trace supplies no runtime result.
